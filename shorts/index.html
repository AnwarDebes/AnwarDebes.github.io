09 · 45 s

An AI denies a loan. What's the smallest change that flips it?

My prototype CCTM (a Tsetlin Machine: a model made of rules you can read) searches for the smallest feature change that flips a loan decision, and writes a receipt that can be re-checked without the model. On synthetic loans with a built-in gender bias, training on one-feature "twins" cut the approval gap from 0.18 to 0.04 on average over 3 seeds; in one seed the twins barely helped.

The catch: the twins were labelled by the bias-free rule that generated the data, and real data has no such oracle. Synthetic data only; draft paper (v0.1), not peer reviewed.

10 · 41 s

Blocking an unsafe AI agent kills the task. Repair the action instead

A blocking guardrail stops an unsafe AI agent action, and the task dies with it. REINS, my runtime guard, tries the edits the deployment allows (add a missing step, swap in a safer tool, drop the call, ask a human) and applies the cheapest one that keeps the policy safe. On my synthetic benchmark (12 seeds, 60 honest and 60 attack tasks per seed) both stopped every attack; blocking finished 32.8% of the honest tasks within the rules, REINS 100%.

That 100% is a best case: the steps these tasks forget are exactly the ones REINS is allowed to add, and without the audit insert it finishes 66.9%. Synthetic tasks, not a live agent. Draft paper.

11 · 46 s

My ARC-AGI-3 agent didn't beat random. My preprint says so.

LUCID is my glass-box AI agent: it plays turn-based games it has never seen, with no instructions, no neural network and no GPU, learns its own readable rules for each game and logs the rule behind every move. On the 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games it scored 0.083% RHAE against 0.095% for random moves, one run each. It does not beat random.

Scope: the public development games, not the official leaderboard; up to 300 actions per game for LUCID and 200 for random; preprint, June 2026.

12 · 39 s

My AI model flagged a molecule. Then it named the bond to cut

A Tsetlin Machine (a model built from readable yes-or-no rules, taught by a GIN neural network) flags a molecule as mutagenic on the TDC AMES test set, then proposes a small graph edit, up to 3, that flips its own prediction: 191 of the first 200 molecules it flagged, 1.30 edits on average. Real data, scaffold split, 729 test molecules, 5 seeds.

Two catches kept in: a fingerprint random forest still predicts better (AUROC 0.790 against my 0.685), and in case 1 the proposed edit detached a carbon while my validity check reported it valid. A flipped prediction is not proof the chemistry changed. Draft paper; not drug-safety advice.

13 · 40 s

I changed one line in a copy of my AI agent's log. It got caught

In Glassbox Studio a small rule-based guard checks every tool call an AI agent proposes, and each decision goes into a ledger where every entry stores a SHA-256 fingerprint of the entry before it. The page's Tamper test edits entry 9 of an 18-entry ledger in a copy, and the check fails at that entry.

The catch: the chain is not signed, so whoever can rewrite the whole ledger can recompute every fingerprint and the check would pass. Prototype; the agent's words are scripted, the guard decisions and both checks are computed live.

14 · 45 s

I retrained my AI guardrail on its own failures. It got worse

A solver (z3) checked 8 must-block words on 8 small prompt-injection guards: 54 of 64 checks failed. After 8 rounds of retraining on those failures, 64 of 64 failed and test accuracy fell from 69.2% to 56.5% (mean of 8 seeds). Hard-coded rules on the starting guards instead: 0 of 64 failed.

The price: those rules block every prompt containing one of the words, one of them is "write", and no harmless test prompt contains any of them, so test accuracy cannot show that cost. Tiny guards, one dataset, 8 seeds, draft paper.

15 · 44 s

I traded 1.5 points of accuracy for an LLM router I can edit

My LLM router is built on a Tsetlin Machine: 4,000 rules vote on where each question goes, and a question with a low vote margin goes to a human instead. On CLINC150 it routes 94.17% of questions correctly (mean of 5 seeds); logistic regression on the same embeddings gets 95.64%. In my edit test, one added rule lifted play_music from 80% to 90%.

The simple baseline is ahead on accuracy, on Banking77 and at spotting off-topic questions. Only 3 of 15 attempted edits improved the test set. Technical report, not peer reviewed; the dataset loaders are not in the repository yet.
